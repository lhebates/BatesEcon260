# The AI Interaction Log — four questions, every time

AI is a welcome partner in your research. The Log is how you show — to me, and more usefully to
yourself — where the tool helped, where it misled you, and where the thinking is yours.

Four questions. Answer them every time you use an AI tool on your project. Two sentences each
is often enough; a bad answer is one that could have been written without opening the tool.

---

### 1. Source audit — *did the tool cite real things, and did I check?*

Paste your exact prompt and the tool's full response. Then take every source it named and find
it: library catalog, Google Scholar, the journal's own site. Mark each one:

- **real and relevant**
- **real but misdescribed** — it exists, but it does not say what the tool claimed
- **not real** — no such paper

The third category is not rare, and it is not a sign the tool is broken. Fabricated citations
look exactly like real ones: plausible authors, plausible journal, plausible year. The only way
to tell is to look.

### 2. Claim audit — *does the summary match what the source actually says?*

Read the source yourself. Then compare it against the tool's summary. Where do they diverge?
Note any factual error, and note anything the summary asserts more confidently than the source
does. Hedges that go missing in summary are the most common failure and the easiest to miss.

### 3. Gap audit — *what did the tool flatten or leave out?*

Summaries compress, and compression is a choice about what matters. What did this one drop?
A qualification, a sample restriction, a competing explanation the authors took seriously, a
limitation they were candid about? What would someone who had only read the summary get wrong?

### 4. Contribution audit — *what here is my judgment, not the tool's?*

The one that matters most. Point at something specific in your work and say: *this decision was
mine.* Choosing this question over that one. Rejecting a source the tool recommended. Reading
the evidence as weaker than it first looked. Deciding where a claim belongs in the argument.

If you cannot answer this one for a piece of work, that is worth knowing before I ask you about
it in class — which I will, and closed-book.

---

## What this is not

It is not a compliance exercise, and it is not there to catch you using AI. It is the record of
how you worked, and the four questions are the same ones a working researcher asks about any
source, human or machine. The audits get sharper as the course goes on; the structure does not
change.

## What it connects to

Your in-class checks are built from **your own** submitted work. A student who can answer
question 4 about their own project can answer questions about it out loud, from memory. That is
the whole design: the Log is the take-home half, and the check is where it becomes yours.
